Podcast Summary | Armchair Expert with Dax Shepard: Eric Goode (documentary filmmaker)

Overview
Eric Goode is a documentary filmmaker whose work on Tiger King, Chimp Crazy, and Monsters of God grew out of a life that has moved through nightlife, hotels, and conservation before turning to film. The throughline is a long-running obsession with animals, especially reptiles and turtles, and an ability to gain access to morally compromised people. His story is less a clean biography than a set of hard-won pivots: clubs taught him to build worlds and networks, hotels gave him resources, conservation gave him purpose, and documentaries gave him a way to chase both truth and dopamine. The result is an argument about extinction, complicity, advocacy, and the uncomfortable empathy required to document people who harm animals.

Key Themes
- Goode rejects neat biographical framing and treats internet summaries as unreliable; his life resists a single arc.
- His parents shaped two competing inheritances: a gay artist father he understood late, and a naturalist mother who taught him to love creatures.
- A Northern California childhood saturated with EST, Esalen, and communes left him over-indoctrinated rather than converted.
- Clubs like Area were creative labs and social cross-pollinators, but rarely lucrative; hotels were the businesses that funded later interests.
- His transition to conservation came from a private, long-held reptile obsession that he kept largely closeted in New York.
- Tiger King's legacy includes real policy change, but effective advocacy requires reaching the unconverted rather than preaching to the already convinced.
- Monsters of God argues that complicity is shared and that unintended consequences of well-meaning laws are central to wildlife crime.
- Animal obsession functions like addiction: it fills a void, supplies self-worth, and can ruin lives.
- Access is the core documentary challenge; danger arrives when subjects feel used or affected by the consequences of being filmed.

Detailed Summary
Eric Goode is presented as someone who has lived what the host calls 25 lives, and he resists the tidy version. He dismisses internet and Wikipedia details as unreliable, joking that peer review is inaccurate too. Born in Rhode Island and later in Georgia, he moved around young, passed through Westchester, New York, and spent formative and high school years in Marin County and Sonoma County. He moved to New York in 1977 and stayed for more than 40 years.

His family was five kids, dysfunctional, and frequently fighting. His father was an artist, a professor, and gay, moving often because he could not hold jobs well. His mother was a naturalist and conservationist, and the household filled with tropical fish, rats, snakes, lizards, dogs, and cats. Goode says he learned a love of creatures from her. The family is from Kerala, India, and Padman is a shortened version of a longer surname changed by his grandfather. He has only been to India once, at age five, though he thought it was age four until his mother corrected him.

The Northern California environment was saturated with EST, Esalen, communes, and figures like Joseph Campbell. Esalen used the family ranch every summer. Goode attended EST around age 15 or 16, where he was told to get on the floor and relive his birth, an experience he describes as chaotic and confusing. He says he rejected the whole milieu because he was over-indoctrinated. He identifies as dyslexic and possibly ADHD, was bad in school, and got kicked out of multiple schools, including his father's alternative high school, the School of Arts and Sciences, for cheating on a geometry test. He attended two art schools, one in San Francisco and Parsons, and says he was kicked out again after the foundation year.

New York nightlife became his real education. He attributes early success to clubs functioning as labs for ideas and cross-pollination, at a time before cell phones, internet, or social media. Area opened in 1983, after Studio 54's owners went to prison, and he says they helped promote Area after getting out. It ran a little less than four years; he notes a three-year run in New York nightlife is like dog years, because clubs are ephemeral. Collaborations included Andy Warhol, Jean-Michel Basquiat, and Keith Haring. He and Basquiat used to do their birthday parties together. He met Basquiat, Haring, and others in the late '70s through the nightclub scene. Drugs then were cocaine, quaaludes, heroin, and alcohol; meth was not an urban drug in the way it is today. He says he was a late bloomer with addiction and had no money early on, so drug use was opportunistic while he focused on surviving. Andy Warhol once asked on Andy Warhol TV what he would do with his money, and Goode said he would get cool tortoises.

He spent a lot of time on the Bowery and started the Maritime. A guest recounts staying at the Maritime Hotel in summer 2005 while working on Baby Mama, eating at Laeta/Loetga, and having a life-affirming moment there. That guest called the hotel operator every morning for coffee and learned she worked in the basement, was a writer for Vice, had a book called Dear Diary, and admired Amy Poehler. The guest brought the book to the Baby Mama set and gave it to Amy Poehler, leading to the writer later writing on Girls and creating her own Netflix show. The operator was Leslie Orphan. Zoe Cassavetes, John Cassavetes's daughter, also worked in the hotel basement for a while.

Hotel ownership surfaced hidden operational realities: deaths by suicide in rooms, housekeeping not entering affected rooms until they are blessed or cleansed, usually by a priest, and reputational effects such as haunted-hotel rumors. At the Maritime, guests told ghost stories, including one woman who said a ghost had sex with her. Goode's grandparents owned the Colonial Motor Inn on the border of Indiana and Michigan, where his grandmother repeatedly found dead bodies over her tenure. The Bowery Hotel keys are large brass fobs with tassels and the room number carved into the brass. The host took a key fob from room 1111 after being told the keys are not for sale but can be charged if taken. Goode also owns Hotel El Roblar in Ojai, which uses brass cowboy hats on the farm that guests take, leading to charges. He owns a tortoise hotel and conservancy in Ojai and says he is better at tortoise hospitality than human hospitality. Hotels were great businesses that afforded resources for other ventures; clubs were fun in youth and not really profitable. He describes a pattern of figuring something out, succeeding, then moving on rather than repeating the same business indefinitely.

Conservation came from a long-held private interest in reptiles and turtles, which he kept largely closeted in New York. He had reptiles and tortoises in his apartment despite social friction. In the early 1980s he incorporated animals into club themes, including a great horned owl, bats, and a 15-foot python that escaped under floorboards and was found years later as a skeleton. He says he slept with a tegu, a large black-and-white South American lizard, in high school. His father was an alcoholic, and he suggests addiction sometimes runs in families. He was with Jean-Michel Basquiat the night Basquiat died of a heroin overdose. He discusses euphoric recall as forgetting the bad parts of addiction and going back.

He cites Elizabeth Kolbert's The Sixth Extinction and describes the current extinction as the first man-made extinction, also called the Anthropocene. He says there are 8 billion people on Earth now, compared with 2.5 billion when he was born, and that primates and turtles or tortoises are the two vertebrate groups most threatened, with over half of each group threatened. He donated $30 million of his own money to turtle conservation, possibly more by now. The Turtle Conservancy successfully bred the plowshare tortoise, described as the rarest tortoise in the world and functionally extinct in the wild in Madagascar, for the first time in the Western world. Thirty young plowshare tortoises are being sent back to Madagascar. He worked with the species for over 20 years before the first hatchling. Leonardo DiCaprio helped with funding.

Tiger King drew 39 million viewers in its first 10 days and faced criticism, but its legacy includes the Big Cat Public Safety Act, which banned private ownership of big cats and big cat puppy mills. Goode credits its accidental origin to a snow leopard being driven in a van during reptile filming in South Florida about 12 years ago. He argues that effective advocacy casts a wide net to reach the unconverted rather than preaching only to the already converted. He identifies as an animal welfare person rather than an animal rights activist, deliberately walking a centrist line and engaging people in red states, because calling people morally repugnant shuts down dialogue.

Monsters of God shows empathy for the masculinity journey of directionless men embraced by motorcycle gangs and drug dealers, and the conclusion is that we are all complicit. It depicts skeletons in zoos' closets, including a man from West Africa exhibited in the Bronx Zoo ape house around 1915 to 1920, humans exhibited in European zoological institutions, and Native Americans in tents at Lincoln Park Zoo in Chicago in the 1950s. He received criticism from the zoo community for showing these historical abuses but argues zoos have evolved and still need to improve. Hank Molt was an intelligent reptile trader who published a list of reptiles for sale; rare species becoming endangered massively increased their value, and his paying clients were mostly zoos like San Diego and Detroit that lacked their own collectors. Hank was imprisoned after a large shipment from Southeast Asia was flagged by customs; the Endangered Species Act and CITES both took effect in 1973. After Hank went to prison, Tommy Crutchfield took over his business and became relatively rich; Hank later got revenge by putting Tommy in prison over Fiji Island iguanas. The Crutchfield-Molt feud is compared to the Joe Exotic-Carole Baskin feud.

Mario was born in Cuba; his father was a CIA operative involved in the Bay of Pigs and later used those skills for drug smuggling in South Florida. Many Miami cocaine cowboys of the '80s had been in the Bay of Pigs operation and resented JFK for the US backing out. Mario smuggled both animals and drugs; his family ran a jewelry store in Little Havana where drug dealers gathered. Janet Reno, then a Florida district attorney, targeted the drug kingpins. Mario killed an undercover informant and burned the body; he was caught and sentenced to 100 years. Before arrest, he had a compound with lions, tigers, monkeys, and reptiles, compared to Pablo Escobar's estate or Scarface, and said his addiction is animals and he sold drugs to fuel it. His release after about 12 years is treated as unexplained. He confided in the filmmaker quickly, including personal details about therapy, an affair with an employee, and motel meetings while his wife was nearby. The filmmaker describes Mario as having sadness and an unbelievable moral code, with mixed feelings about him.

Anson Wong is described as a mythical figure and the Pablo Escobar of the wildlife trade, based in Penang, Malaysia, operating for 30 to 40 years. He previously had access to major zoos and people in the United States, including Tom Schultz at the San Diego Zoo, until he was wanted for smuggling. He was eventually arrested and received one of the longest wildlife-crime sentences ever in the 1990s, reportedly seven or eight years, after the government spent millions to get him. The US government ran a five-year undercover operation; after prison he returned to Malaysia, was banned from the US, and was later arrested again in Malaysia for reptile smuggling. Fear around Wong was pervasive: Malaysian fixers avoided contacting him, and the filmmaker eventually reached him only by repeatedly calling a Malaysian number. The filmmaker deliberately delayed intervening on Tonka to pursue the larger source, framing it like drug enforcement: not just catching a small dealer, but trying to get the source.

Animal obsession and hoarding are framed as analogous to addiction, with the same pattern of filling a void, self-worth, validation, and life-ruining behavior. Coverage of animal suffering is balanced against audience tolerance: too much brutality makes viewers turn away, so the approach keeps it entertaining even amid serious subject matter. After Chimp Crazy wrapped, Tanya Hadex got another chimp in her basement and is now in prison for it. The owner of Travis tried to get another baby monkey and did get another chimp. Goode communicated with primatologists about Tonka, including Craig Stanford at USC and Russell Mittermeier. Goode says he tried to counsel Tanya Hadex after Chimp Crazy, encouraging contrition and telling her not to keep repeating the same behavior. Both hosts describe recurring using dreams about cocaine, specifically dreams involving getting cocaine out of carpet and dealing with lint. Goode says using dreams are intense and panic-inducing, but waking up brings relief.
