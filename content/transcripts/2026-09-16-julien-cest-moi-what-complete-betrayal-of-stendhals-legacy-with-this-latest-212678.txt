“Stendhal, the enemy of hypocrites, (…) treats himself as a hypocrite in the conclusion of The Red and the Black. (…) The novelist recognizes himself as guilty of the sin of which he accuses his mediator. The curse that Oedipus launched against the Others falls back on his own head. It is this curse that Flaubert’s famous cry expresses: ‘Mme Bovary, c’est moi!’ Mme Bovary was first conceived as that contemptible Other whom Flaubert had sworn to settle accounts with. Mme Bovary is first Flaubert’s enemy as Julien Sorel is Stendhal’s enemy (…). But the novel’s hero, without ever ceasing to be the Other, gradually joins the novelist in the course of creation. When Flaubert cries ‘Mme Bovary, c’est moi,’ he does not mean that Mme Bovary is henceforth one of those flattering doubles with which romantic writers love to surround themselves. He means that the Self and the Other become one in the novelistic miracle. Great novelistic creations are always the fruit of a fascination that has been overcome. (…) The task is all the more difficult because the novelist himself sometimes tries to mislead us. Stendhal attributes Julien Sorel’s ‘German mysticism’ to an overly damp dungeon. The conclusion of The Red and the Black nevertheless remains a meditation on Christian themes and symbols. The novelist reaffirms his skepticism there, but the themes and symbols are no less present for being wrapped in negations.”

Yves Grava 

I propose (…) to re-examine the case of Stendhal’s Le Rouge et le Noir (…) on the occasion of the broadcast on France Télévisions of a four-episode series that adapts the novel: an object I find half-successful, but is the source material really that good? – Le Rouge et le Noir: it is the story of a young man from an artisan family in the Franche-Comté of 1828. Of weak constitution and vaguely intellectual, he is destined for the priesthood when his father sends him to be tutor to the children of the mayor of Verrières, a certain Monsieur de Rênal. Frustrated by nature, fiery only when he feels he has the means, Julien quickly tries to seduce the mistress of the house, who resists weakly, then no longer at all, until the rumor of her infidelity circulates and Julien is forced to leave the village. He then goes to Besançon, where he enters the seminary, then to Paris, where he quickly seduces a young noblewoman, Mathilde de La Mole, whose hand he almost obtains, until Madame de Rênal, jealous, denounces him throughout the region as a hypocrite and seducer. Julien, mad with rage, shoots his former mistress, appears before a court, out of pride does not defend himself, and dies on the guillotine. (…) what strikes me on rereading is how dry Le Rouge et le Noir is, with a strange rhythm that dwells on some episodes and skips others with the same casualness. (…) Besides the historical didacticism, the series seems to yield to several French passions: developing a secondary servant character to draw a love triangle; freezing Julien Sorel in the posture of a domestic when in the novel he is from the start in a kind of intermediate social space; correcting the misogyny of the text, or believing it does so by making Madame de Rênal a passionate and self-assured woman when Stendhal makes her a child-woman full of inconsistencies. Above all, the series transforms the heroes into romantic characters, drowning the carnal encounters in music, showing Julien contemplating the Jura like Caspar David Friedrich’s Wanderer with Schubert on the soundtrack, and thus erasing Stendhal’s very particular irony. An irony that constantly degrades the characters, and Julien in particular: choleric rather than wounded, despising the aristocracy less from opinion than from frustration, who works less for the interests of his class than for his own fate alone, and for whom idealism, very moderate, is a form of opportunism. On rereading Le Rouge et le Noir, I find the characters quite unsympathetic and this representation of the world particularly cruel, at times bordering on a superior cynicism that is not even supported by the rigor of the narrative, and finally I understand the series’ obvious intention to lift them out of mediocrity. And then, as far as irony is concerned, it is said, I prefer Flaubert.
