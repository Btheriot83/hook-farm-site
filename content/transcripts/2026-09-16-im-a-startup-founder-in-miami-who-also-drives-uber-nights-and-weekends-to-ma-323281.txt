I’m a startup founder in Miami who also drives Uber nights and weekends to make ends meet. @Uber , I want the platform to succeed. But I don’t think we’re measuring driver economics correctly.

I reviewed 7 recent UberX rides from my own account. Excluding tips and toll reimbursements:

57.03 passenger-trip miles
2 hours 4 minutes with riders
$62.28 in driver earnings before vehicle expenses

If you look only at pickup-to-drop-off time, that appears to be about $30/hour gross.

But that’s not a driver’s working clock.

Our time starts when we accept the ride and begin driving to the passenger.

If the drive to those 7 pickups averaged just 10 minutes each, my active time is already at least 3 hours 14 minutes, and the same $62.28 becomes about $19.26/hour gross.

And that’s before counting additional pickup waiting.

It’s also before energy, depreciation, tires, maintenance, cleaning and insurance.

And it still excludes time online between rides and repositioning after a drop-off.

Uber itself has used a definition of “active time” that includes driving to pickup, waiting and completing the trip.

There’s another problem: paid wait time generally doesn’t begin until we reach the designated pickup point and the rider’s grace period expires.

In Miami, real life isn’t always that clean.

At a gated community, I can be sitting at the gate waiting for access while the app doesn’t consider me at the pickup.

At an event, reaching a passenger can take 20–30+ minutes.

If traffic or police make me circle while the rider is still inside, I’m still committing my time and vehicle to that ride, but the paid-wait clock may not reflect it.

My receipt shows paid waiting at $0.19/minute, or $11.40/hour, once that clock is actually running.

Here’s another benchmark that caught my attention:

NYC’s 2026 minimum Uber/Lyft driver-pay formula is $1.283/mile + $0.681/minute.

Apply that formula to the same 57.03 passenger miles and 124 passenger minutes and the minimum comes to about $157.61.

My actual earnings on those Miami rides were $62.28.

NYC is a different market and more expensive overall. I’m not saying Miami should copy NYC.

But a ~2.5x difference is large enough to ask whether Miami’s driver economics are calibrated correctly.

And this matters competitively.

Driver-first alternatives are targeting exactly this pain point. Empower launched in Miami this year with a model that lets drivers set their own rates and keep the fare while paying the platform separately. Epic Rides is local and signing hundreds of drivers per week.

I’m already seeing riders and drivers experiment with alternatives because cancellations, long pickup times and difficulty getting rides accepted hurt both sides.

Uber has an enormous liquidity advantage today.

But if better economics start pulling driver supply elsewhere, the consequences show up as longer ETAs and more cancellations. Eventually riders follow the supply.

That’s how a driver-economics problem can become a market-share problem.

I’m not arguing Uber should simply take less margin.

I’m not arguing riders should simply pay dramatically more.

I’m arguing that better driver economics can also mean more reliable supply, shorter pickups, fewer cancellations, less deadhead mileage and potentially less money required to incentivize supply.

That can be good for riders, drivers AND shareholders.

This is a small sample from my own driving, not a claim that 7 rides represent all of Miami.

I’m using it to illustrate what I think is a measurement problem. @Uber : I’d genuinely like to see Miami driver economics published using total active time, actual pickup time, realistic vehicle costs and tips separated.

That’s the number that matters.

I’m not someone who comes with a problem without a solution.

I put what I’d test in the next post.
