## Transcript (~30s)

She said the first thing she does before every ride — as a passenger — is check both apps. Every single time. No exceptions.

Uber and Lyft run completely separate pricing algorithms. They have different driver pools. They have different surge calculations. They have different base rates in many markets. At any given moment, one app can be 20–40% cheaper than the other for the exact same ride.

"I open Uber. I check the fare estimate. I open Lyft. I check the fare estimate. It takes 15 seconds. On a typical ride in a mid-size city, the difference is $3–$8. On a surge night — Friday evening, bar close, after a concert — the difference can be $15–$25. One app is surging 2.5x while the other is at 1.0x because their driver supplies are completely independent. The riders who use only one app are overpaying on roughly 40% of their rides."

She said most people download one app and never open the other.

"They pick Uber or Lyft — usually whichever one they downloaded first — and they use it exclusively. They never comparison shop. They never check the other app. They assume the prices are the same. They're not. They're often dramatically different. The algorithm doesn't know what the other app is charging. Each app is pricing against its own supply and demand — not against the competitor."

She said the math is brutal for single-app riders over a year.

"If you take 3 rides per week and the average overpay on the rides where the other app is cheaper is $5, that's $780/year in unnecessary spending. For 5 rides per week, it's $1,300. Just from not opening a second app for 15 seconds before pressing 'confirm.' That's not a hack. That's basic price comparison. And most riders never do it."
